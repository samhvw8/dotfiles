# Audio and podcast

Load for a podcast script, a voice-over or any spoken-word audio. Grades (`S`, `A`, `B`, `H`), defaults and checks are in [../SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). Hook and wording rules are in [hooks-and-wording.md](hooks-and-wording.md). Loudness targets and chapter rules are in [platform-specs.md](platform-specs.md); read it only for a production handoff. For video podcasts, also read [video-and-motion.md](video-and-motion.md).

Audio is linear: the listener cannot skim back to a heading. So the script carries its own structure, and the first 30 s gives a reason to stay.

## Before you judge a script

- **Build a timeline.** Count the words (English) or syllables (Vietnamese) in each block, divide by the speaker's own rate, and note what the listener has received by each timestamp. Rank the problems from this timeline: dead time before the first content, and where the best material sits.
- **Check the total against what the user said.** When the timed length differs from the length they named, tell them.
- **Find the best material.** The strongest hook is usually already in the script, later on: the host's own story, a real number, the surprising claim. Move it to the first line.
- **Check the sponsor.** Does the sponsor fit this episode's topic, or pull against it? A tension can be turned into the read or flagged; do not ignore it. Before you move a sponsor read, tell the user to check what the contract says about placement.

## Rules

| Rule | Grade and source | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold open on the content.** Start on the story moment, the claim or the question the episode answers. Show name, theme, thanks, sponsor and "follow the show" come after the hook; the ask to follow or rate goes at the end, after the listener got something | H: practitioner consensus | No controlled study. Regular listeners still know the show by the voice |
| **Talk, don't announce.** A conversational, narrative delivery with pitch movement on key words holds attention and recall better than a steady announcer cadence | B: Rodero & Cores-Sarría 2023; Rodero & Larrea 2021 (Spanish samples, news and ads) | — |
| **Time at the speaker's rate** (SKILL speech-rate default). Advise slowing only on numbers, dense ideas and the key line | B | Never mark a natural pace as a fault |
| **One deliberate pause before the key line.** A short line set alone after a pause lands harder | H | Once or twice per episode. Keep room tone under it; digital silence sounds like a dropout |
| **Say the structure out loud.** Announce the parts ("Three reasons. First…") and reuse the same ordinal words at each transition | A: signaling principle (Schneider et al. 2018 meta-analysis, mostly multimedia learning) | Keep the count near 3; listeners cannot see the list. Group a longer list into about 3 parts |
| **Reset the room.** Every few minutes in a long episode, say in one sentence where the argument stands and what comes next | H | A recap, not a repeat |
| **Alternate idea and scene.** After a general claim, tell one concrete scene with a name, a place and a number; then return to the idea | A: concreteness and narrative transportation (van Laer et al. 2014) | The scene must show the same point, or it is a tangent (G10) |
| **One real question beats a string of them.** Ask the question the listener cannot answer at once and the episode answers; then start answering | H | Several rhetorical questions in a row sound like filler |
| **Numbers are real and sourced.** Say where a statistic comes from in a few words; "studies show" is not a source. When you cut an unsourced figure, offer a real one only if you can name its source, and mark it to verify before recording. The host's own number often does the job better | A (G14, G18) | A Tier 3 claim in the script is a finding to report: say which replication failed, and offer a line the host can say honestly from experience |
| **A specific promise.** Tell the listener what they will have by the end, and make sure the episode delivers it | H | Do not change what the episode promises without saying so |
| **End on the payoff.** The last segment resolves the opening question and gives one action. Keep the outro short | H; A for peak-end | — |

## The rewrite

Write the opening in full, as the host will say it, in the host's own voice and pronouns, with natural spoken rhythm (short lines, a beat of silence where it lands). Give a timestamp per block, computed at the host's rate, and the total length. Use delivery cues sparingly (`[pause]`, `[theme, under 10 s]`, `[sponsor read]`); a list of cues is not a script. Keep the host's real details; where a detail is missing, write the line so it works without it or bracket it once.

## Common failures

- The first 20–40 s spent on greetings, thanks, sponsor and "remember to follow".
- A string of rhetorical questions with obvious answers before any content.
- An unsourced statistic, or a debunked study, used as support.
- A long list of tips announced as a long list.
- The story that would make the best hook buried a minute in.
